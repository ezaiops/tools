Unix time in one paragraph

The Unix epoch is the number of seconds elapsed since 00:00:00 UTC on January 1, 1970 (leap seconds excluded — Unix time pretends every day has exactly 86,400 seconds). Millisecond timestamps are the same count multiplied by 1,000, which is what JavaScript's Date.now() and most JSON APIs return. A timestamp of 0 is therefore the epoch itself, and negative values are moments before 1970.

Common timestamp pitfalls

  • Seconds vs milliseconds. A 10-digit timestamp is seconds; 13 digits is milliseconds. Mixing them is the single most common timestamp bug — 13-digit values overflow 32-bit ints and break some databases.
  • Second-resolution storage. Storing a float like 1728790800.123 silently drops sub-second precision in many engines.
  • Year 2038. 32-bit signed seconds overflow on January 19, 2038. Use 64-bit storage or milliseconds.
  • Server timezone is not your timezone. convert in UTC and format at the edge; never format a timestamp in the timezone of whatever VM happens to run your code.

Frequently asked questions

What is the maximum timestamp?
JavaScript dates are reliable up to ±8,640,000,000,000,000 ms around the epoch — roughly ±273,790 years, far beyond any practical use. 64-bit systems share a similar limit.
Are leap seconds counted?
No. Unix time ignores leap seconds; UTC and Unix clocks drift apart by a second each time a leap second is inserted, and are re-synchronized on the next. For logging purposes the difference is invisible.
Does this send my timestamps anywhere?
No. All conversions happen in your browser with the built-in Date object.
